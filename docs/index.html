Every pricing project starts the same way: fit a demand curve to your sales history, find the profit-maximising price, ship the dashboard. We did that. Then we checked whether the answer was real — and it wasn't.

This system does the honest version instead: it proves the historical data is contaminated, refuses to guess where it can't know, and then closes the loop — running deliberate price experiments, measuring the response, updating its beliefs, and re-optimising.

STEP 1 Your sales data is confounded

Prices go UP when the store gets busy — so price and demand are tangled

Umbrella prices rise when it rains. Umbrella sales rise when it rains. Your data now says "high price → high sales". The rain caused both, and "rain" is not a column in the spreadsheet.

Measured in this store: across the full transaction base, prices are +6.3% higher in the busiest weeks than the quietest — exactly when demand peaks. (On the eligible panel — the products with the most price movement, where the confounding is mildest — it is still +2.4%.) 63% of products have price positively correlated with store traffic (random pricing would give ~50%).

And there's barely any demand to explain: the median product sells 2 units a week; 62% of product-weeks sell 2 or fewer.
The wreckage: 38% of products come out with a POSITIVE elasticity — customers apparently buying more as the price rises. Median estimate -0.61 against a published retail benchmark of −2.62 (Bijmolt, Van Heerde & Pieters, 2005 — 1,851 elasticities, 81 studies).

The baseline test that settled it

Held out the last 12 weeks. Given the price actually charged in a week the model never saw, predict the units that actually sold.

STEP 3 So you have to TEST

A deliberate price change is the only thing that breaks the confounding

The loop moves a price on purpose — not because the store is busy, not because it's Christmas, but to find out. That single act severs the link between price and the hidden reasons demand moves. It manufactures the missing ingredient your history never had.

Then it observes what sold, updates its belief, re-optimises, and does it again. Act → observe → learn → re-optimise. My first two projects stopped at "decide" and never found out whether they were right. This one does.

ORACLE — knows the truth
The ceiling. The prize is real.
The loop just can't reach it in two years when products sell 2 units a week.
20 independent replications, 104 weeks, 30 products. Elasticity error fell from 2.09 to 1.32 — the system corrects itself.

Volume is the currency of experimentation

You cannot price-test a product nobody buys. With 2 units a week, random fluctuation swamps a 10% demand change — you'd need years to hear the signal.

low volume (<3 units/wk): final elasticity error 1.27
high volume (>8 units/wk): final elasticity error 0.61 — half

The operator's entry condition: run price tests on the products that actually move. Leave the long tail alone. That is where the +10.2% prize is reachable.

What's real and what's simulated

REAL — measured from the data
  • Every price, unit and product — 131,706 real transactions
  • The confounding evidence (prices +2.4% in busy weeks)
  • The elasticity estimates and their failure against benchmark
  • The baseline test on held-out weeks
  • Each product's price, price range, baseline demand and Poisson noise
  • The loop's starting belief — the real, confounded estimate a business walks in with
SIMULATED — and it has to be
  • The demand response to prices never charged.
  • If a store never sold that product at £8.20, no dataset on earth contains how many units it would have sold at £8.20. Counterfactuals aren't observable.
  • So each product is given a true elasticity (drawn near the −2.62 benchmark) and the loop must discover it.
  • This is the load-bearing assumption of the loop. It does not prove "these are the right prices." It proves the mechanism works: a system starting from a wrong belief converges on the truth and beats trusting your data.
